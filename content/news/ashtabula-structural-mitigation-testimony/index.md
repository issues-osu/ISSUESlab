Dr. **Gia Barboza-Salerno** was featured in the *Star Beacon* for her expert witness testimony on **structural mitigation** during a capital resentencing trial in Ashtabula County, Ohio.

In her testimony, Dr. Barboza-Salerno—serving as a social scientist and statistics expert—presented spatial and empirical evidence on how neighborhood-level structural conditions, including chronic unemployment, under-resourced school systems, historical inequities, and lack of institutional support, compound individual developmental vulnerabilities and trauma across the life course.

As Dr. Barboza-Salerno explained to the court, examining structural and societal context does not excuse or justify harm, but is essential for understanding the cumulative systemic failures that shape developmental trajectories:

> *"At every turn, you could point to one of many systemic failures."*

### Full Article Coverage (*Star Beacon*)

**Testimony continues in Jones resentencing trial**  
*By Christopher Gillett (`cgillett@starbeacon.com`)*  
*Published October 21, 2026 — Star Beacon (Ashtabula, OH)*

JEFFERSON — Jurors heard from three witnesses Tuesday as the resentencing trial for Odraye Jones continued.

Jones, who changed his name to Malik Allah U Akbar while in prison, is being resentenced for the Nov. 17, 1997 murder of Ashtabula Police Officer William D. Glover Jr.

Jones’ 1998 death sentence was vacated on federal habeas review due to racist statements made by a clinical psychologist who testified for the defense. The conviction stands.

Glover, a 30-year-old patrolman and father of three, went to a home in the 900 block of West 43rd Street in Ashtabula to arrest Jones on a robbery warrant.

The resentencing trial started Monday, where both sides gave opening arguments.

Prosecutors said they were still interested in pursuing the death penalty for Jones, and the defense asked the jury to impose a life sentence.

The state called retired Ashtabula Police Officer Jim Oatman as its sole witness Monday, who said Jones had told friends he would “shoot a police officer before being arrested.”

Oatman also identified evidence, played dispatch recordings and presented other related exhibits, during the course of his testimony.

After the state rested, the defense called Ashtabula County Jail Administrator Tammy Antoun, who said Jones had been respectful and behaved in the county jail, but she does not have day-to-day contact with him.

All three expert witnesses Tuesday were introduced by defense attorney Donald Malarcik.

Forensic psychologist Dr. Bob Stinson continued testimony he started Monday.

He wrapped up his presentation on several mitigating factors in Jones’ life.

Stinson said Jones witnessed domestic violence against his mother, his cousin die in a shooting, had to travel to the emergency room on his own as a child for medical emergencies, suffered trauma from an assault a few years before Glover’s death and had a lack of structure and consistency over who was taking care of him and in his education.

Jones was sent to the Ohio Department of Youth Services, when he was there in the 1990s, which was later sued by the federal government on constitutional grounds, he said.

The accumulation of all these factors together was what mattered, Stinson said, likening it to stacking weights onto someone and asking them to walk further and further. “If we kind of look at the chronology of events we get a real clear indication of the just chaos and inconsistency and lack of structure that really permeated Malik’s life,” Stinson said.

Representing the state, Dan Kasaris cross examined Stinson.

He pointed out Jones had personally declined a psychological interview with Stinson, and his only interaction with him had been a roughly five minute phone call. He asked how important an interview would have been.

Stinson said one-on-one interviews with a subject can be important, but are not always, because the subject can tell the interviewer incorrect things.

Often, forensic psychologists rely on interviews from multiple people related to the subject, and would look for a convergence of statements to find out the truth, he said.

Kasaris said there are many people like Jones who have dealt with mitigating factors and have not killed anyone.

Stinson said the mitigating factors did not strip Jones of his free will, but did send him down a path where he was more likely to commit a crime.

Kasaris also had Stinson read out multiple files from Jones’ incarceration where he was cited for improper actions.

Kasaris brought up past testimony in the Jones case from the late neurosurgeon Dr. Robert White, who had said Jones’ head injury was insignificant.

Stinson said it may not have been major from the perspective of a neurosurgeon who performed around 10,000 surgeries, like White, but it would be serious for any normal person.

Neuropsychologist Dr. Jeff Madden also spoke to the court more specifically about the potential effects of the head trauma on Jones.

He said recent scans indicated severe long-term brain damage to Jones.

Madden said Jones declined to meet with him, and he worried that Jones suffered from anosognosia, not realizing the extent of his potential impairment.

In his cross-examination, Kasaris asked Madden if the damage shown on scans would be there when Glover was killed.

Madden said it would not have, but he still would have faced impairment.

Kasaris asked Madden about White’s testimony again.

Madden said White’s claim was nonsense.

“I’m from New Jersey, so I’m highly opinionated, but that’s one of the most ridiculous things I’ve ever seen,” he said.

The last witness the jury heard from Tuesday was social scientist and statistics expert **Gia Barboza-Salerno**, who focused on the societal factors that influenced Jones’ upbringing.

She said the neighborhood Jones grew up in struggled with a lot of issues, including unemployment, a struggling school system, historic inequalities and other factors that influenced whether he could find support.

Barboza-Salerno said the factors stacked up on top of his development as he dealt with other issues like the head trauma.

Prosecutor Kara Keating led the state’s cross examination, arguing Barboza-Salerno did not account enough for Jones’ individual life and resources he had available through youth sports, church, the YMCA and the nearby hospital.

She said Barboza-Salerno did not interview Jones and asked what he thought of his foster grandmother, whether he considered her a good role model or if he had other role models in his life.

Barboza-Salerno said people of any age can consider someone a good role model, regardless of how true that is.

She said science is concerned with generalities and not specifics.

The societal factors do not justify the crime, but help describe what led to it, Barboza-Salerno said.

“At every turn, you could point to one of many systemic failures,” she said.
