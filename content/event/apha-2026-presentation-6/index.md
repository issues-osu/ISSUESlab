abstract: "Opioid-related overdose is a public health crisis, shaped by socio-built environmental factors. Overdose has increased in rural areas; however, commonly used census-based classifications overlook fine-grained variations across areas, which obscure local variation, settlement patterns, and introduce ecological bias. We address this using remote sensing data, capturing built environment features to examine fatal opioid overdoses. We performed a retrospective ecological study analyzing fatal opioid overdoses within census block groups in Cook County, Illinois, from 2018 to 2023. Urbanicity was classified using satellite data to measure environmental indicators (built-up intensity, vegetative greenness, nighttime light intensity (NLI)), then combined with census-based measures of neighborhood deprivation to characterize physical and social conditions. A Bayesian spatiotemporal model was estimated, yielding neighborhood-level estimates of overdose risk. There were 9,249 opioid-related deaths, with clustering and strong associations with socio-built environmental factors. Neighborhood disadvantage had a dose-response relationship, with high deprivation areas experiencing over four times the risk of overdose. NLI had a 10.24-fold increase in overdose mortality, while vegetative greenness showed no significant protective effects. Urban areas had the highest absolute risk, yet increasing temporal trends occurred across all regions, including 25% of rural and transitional areas. Remote sensing data provides more granular and consistent characterization of the socio-built environment, enabling public health officials and urban planners to identify high-risk environments and implement place-based interventions."
