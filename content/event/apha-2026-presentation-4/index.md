abstract: "Climate change is increasing the frequency, intensity, and duration of extreme heat events, with important implications for population mental health. Older adults are disproportionately affected by heat-related risks due to physiological vulnerability, social isolation, and limited adaptive capacity, yet suicide outcomes remain underexamined within climate–mental health research. We conducted an ecological longitudinal time-series study of suicide mortality among adults aged ≥65 years in Chicago, Illinois, from 2016–2024. Daily suicide deaths were linked to ambient temperature data to identify extreme heat events, defined as temperatures exceeding the 75th percentile threshold. Neighborhood deprivation was measured using area-based indices and linked to decedents' residential locations. Poisson regression models estimated associations between extreme heat exposure and suicide mortality, incorporating distributed lag structures and adjusting for neighborhood deprivation, decedent demographics, seasonal patterns, and temporal trends. Extreme heat events were associated with a statistically significant increase in suicide mortality among older adults, with the strongest effect observed at a two-day lag. Each 1°C increase above the extreme heat threshold corresponded to an estimated 20% increase in suicide risk. Extreme heat exposure and neighborhood deprivation independently contributed to suicide mortality."
